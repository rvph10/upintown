A quote sent from a Gmail or Hotmail address works perfectly well. But when a customer is choosing between two businesses, an address like contact@your-business.com shows at a glance that yours is established. It is a small detail that builds trust, and you only have to set it up once. Here is what you need, what it costs and how to go about it.

## What is a professional email address?

It is an address ending in your business name rather than a provider's: contact@your-business.com instead of your.business@gmail.com.

It reminds people of your business with every message, and it matches your website, your Google Business Profile and your business cards. It also stays the same if you ever change email provider, because what counts is your domain name, not the tool behind it.

## Which address should you choose?

Keep it short and easy to say over the phone. A few common forms:

- **contact@** or **info@** for a general address to show on your website
- **firstname@** for a more personal feel, useful if you work alone
- **quotes@** or **invoices@** to keep certain conversations apart

For a small business, one mailbox is often enough. An extra address can simply be an alias, a second name that lands in the same mailbox.

## Can you get one for free?

Partly. A @gmail.com address is free, but it shows Gmail's name rather than yours. For an address in your own name you need a domain, and that is paid for every year.

The mailbox itself can be free. Zoho, for example, has [a free plan](https://www.zoho.com/mail/zohomail-pricing.html) that lets you use your own domain, for up to five users. It has limits: there is no IMAP or POP, the two methods that let you read your email in a program such as Outlook. If you are happy to work in the provider's browser page or app, that may be all you need. Check the conditions when you choose, as they do change.

## What it really costs

There are two parts to the cost:

- **The domain name.** A .be domain, for instance, is bought from [a registrar rather than from DNS Belgium](https://www.dnsbelgium.be/en/our-role/registry-registrar-registrant), the body that runs that extension. Each registrar sets its own price, so compare the renewal price and not just the first year.
- **The email service.** This is usually charged per mailbox, per month. As a guide, Google Workspace's entry plan was [listed in Belgium at €6.80 per user per month before tax](https://workspace.google.com/intl/fr_be/pricing) in November 2026, before any promotion.

The real hidden cost lies elsewhere: the time spent on setup, and the messages that go astray if it is done badly.

## How to set up your address, step by step

This is the usual sequence, whichever provider you pick.

1. **Register your domain name**, if you don't have one yet. Put it in your own name or your business's name, not a supplier's.
2. **Choose an email service**: Zoho Mail, Google Workspace or Microsoft 365, for example.
3. **Prove the domain is yours.** The provider gives you a line to add to the domain's settings, known as DNS.
4. **Point your mail at the service.** That is the job of MX records, further lines in the same settings.
5. **Add the authentication settings**, explained below.
6. **Create your addresses**, then test by sending and receiving a message from another account.

## The settings that keep you out of spam folders

When you send an email, your customer's mail service checks that it really came from you. To help it, your domain can carry three settings called SPF, DKIM and DMARC. Put simply, they are signatures proving your messages aren't imitations.

Gmail [requires at least SPF or DKIM from all senders](https://support.google.com/a/answer/81126?hl=en), and all three above 5,000 messages a day. It recommends all three to everyone, even at low volumes.

Without them, your quotes and invoices may end up in your customers' spam folders, with nobody telling you. If you already have a professional address and some customers aren't getting your messages, this is the first thing to check.

## Switching without losing anything

Moving from an old address to the new one takes a little preparation:

- **Import your old messages** into the new mailbox, so your history stays in one place.
- **Forward the old address** to the new one for a few months, while everyone gets used to it.
- **Tell your regular customers** and add the new address to your signature.
- **Update your address everywhere**: website, [Google Business Profile and directories](/en/blog/local-google-visibility), social media, invoices and business cards.

Keep the old address active for as long as messages still arrive there.

## Do it yourself or hand it over?

If DNS settings don't put you off and you have a little time to spare, you can do all of this on your own, as the providers publish detailed guides. For a single mailbox, that is a perfectly sound choice.

Handing it over makes sense if you would rather not touch those settings, if you have a lot of old mail to bring across, or if you want someone to contact on the day a message goes missing.
